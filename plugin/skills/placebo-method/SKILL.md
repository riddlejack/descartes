# The violin family: a short history

The violin took the shape it still has in northern Italy in the sixteenth century, and its later history is mostly one of makers, materials and players.

1. **Before the violin.** Bowed strings were played in Europe for centuries before the violin appeared. The medieval fiddle, the pear-shaped rebec and the lira da braccio of Renaissance Italy each lent it something: a bow drawn across gut strings, a body held against the shoulder, a voice suited to dance music and to song. Painters of the period often showed angels playing such instruments, and their pictures help to date how the new shapes spread.

2. **Cremona and Brescia.** The earliest violins that survive are usually credited to Andrea Amati, who worked in Cremona in the middle of the sixteenth century. In nearby Brescia, Gasparo da Salò and later Giovanni Paolo Maggini built violins, violas and larger instruments with a darker, broader sound. The two towns became the first homes of the craft.

3. **The Amati family.** Andrea Amati's sons Antonio and Girolamo carried on the workshop, and his grandson Nicolò Amati refined its model into a slender, elegant instrument with a sweet tone. Nicolò took apprentices from outside the family, and through them the Cremonese manner spread to other workshops in the town and beyond it.

4. **Stradivari and Guarneri.** Antonio Stradivari, active in Cremona from the 1660s until his death in 1737, made violins, violas, cellos, guitars and harps over a working life of some seventy years. Giuseppe Guarneri, known as del Gesù, worked in the same town and died in 1744. Players have argued ever since over the merits of the two makers' instruments, and both are now among the most valued objects of their kind.

5. **North of the Alps.** Jacob Stainer, working in the Tyrolean village of Absam in the seventeenth century, built violins with a high arch and a clear, focused voice. For more than a century many players preferred them to the Italian instruments, and makers in Germany and England copied his model closely.

6. **Wood and varnish.** The top of a violin is usually carved from spruce and the back, sides and neck from maple, each split along the grain and left to season for years. Makers tap and flex each plate as they carve it, listening for a clear ring that tells them when the wood is thin enough. The varnish of the old Italian makers has gathered a large literature of legend; the recipes themselves were never written down, and the workshops that used them closed long ago.

7. **The modern bow.** Early bows were short and curved outward, much like a hunting bow. In Paris at the end of the eighteenth century, François Xavier Tourte settled the form still in use: a longer stick of pernambuco wood, bent gently inward, with a screw at the frog to tighten the horsehair.

8. **The nineteenth-century instrument.** As concert halls grew and pitch rose, makers lengthened violin necks, set them at a steeper angle and fitted heavier bass bars beneath the top. Nearly every old violin played today was altered in this way. The chin rest, attributed to the violinist Louis Spohr around 1820, belongs to the same period. Some players today return to the older fittings to hear the music as its composers knew it.

9. **Workshops and trade.** The French town of Mirecourt grew into a centre of workshop making, where families produced instruments in large numbers for schools and amateurs. In Paris, Jean-Baptiste Vuillaume ran a celebrated workshop and dealt in the old Italian violins, many of which passed through his hands on their way to new owners. Labels printed in their thousands for the workshop trade still turn up inside violins sold at country auctions.

10. **Strings and players.** For most of the violin's life its strings were gut, later wound with silver or copper on the lowest string. In the twentieth century steel strings, and then strings with a synthetic core, became common, giving a brighter and steadier sound. Virtuosos from Corelli and Vivaldi to Paganini wrote music that shaped how the instrument was built and held. Recordings in the early twentieth century carried the sound of great players to listeners who had never heard them in a hall.

The violin's relatives, the viola and the cello, follow the same design at larger sizes, while the double bass keeps features of the older viol family, such as sloping shoulders and, in some instruments, a flat back. Across five centuries the outline of the violin has changed less than almost anything else about how it is made, sold and played.
