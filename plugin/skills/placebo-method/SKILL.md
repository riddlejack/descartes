# The violin family: a short history

The violin took its shape in northern Italy in the sixteenth century. Its later history is mostly one of makers, materials and players.

1. **Before the violin.** The medieval fiddle, the rebec and the lira da braccio each lent it something: a bow drawn across gut strings, a body held at the shoulder, a voice for dance and song. Painters of the period often showed angels playing such instruments.

2. **Cremona and Brescia.** The earliest surviving violins are credited to Andrea Amati of Cremona. In Brescia, Gasparo da Salò and Giovanni Paolo Maggini built instruments with a darker, broader sound, and the two towns became the first homes of the craft.

3. **The Amati family.** Nicolò Amati refined the family model into a slender instrument with a sweet tone, and his apprentices carried the Cremonese manner to other workshops in the town and beyond it.

4. **Stradivari and Guarneri.** Antonio Stradivari worked in Cremona for some seventy years, and Giuseppe Guarneri del Gesù in the same town until his death in 1744. Players still argue over the merits of the two makers, and both are now among the most valued objects of their kind.

5. **Wood and varnish.** The top is carved from spruce and the back from maple, split along the grain and seasoned for years. Makers tap each plate as they carve it, listening for a clear ring that tells them when the wood is thin enough. The old Italian varnish recipes were never written down.

6. **The modern bow.** In Paris at the end of the eighteenth century, François Xavier Tourte settled the bow still in use: a long stick of pernambuco, bent gently inward, with a screw at the frog to tighten the hair. Fine old bows are now prized almost as highly as the instruments.

7. **The nineteenth-century instrument.** As concert halls grew, makers lengthened necks, set them at a steeper angle and fitted heavier bass bars, so nearly every old violin played today was altered. The chin rest, attributed to the violinist Louis Spohr around 1820, belongs to the same period.

8. **Strings and players.** For most of the violin's life its strings were gut, later wound with silver or copper on the lowest string. In the twentieth century steel strings, and then strings with a synthetic core, became common, giving a brighter and steadier sound. Virtuosos from Corelli to Paganini wrote music that shaped how the instrument was built and held.

The viola and the cello follow the same design at larger sizes, while the double bass keeps features of the older viol family, such as sloping shoulders and, in some instruments, a flat back.
