# Framing and decomposing the question

Think the frame and tree through in prose in frame.md first; enter the tree with tree_upsert only once the prose holds together.

1. **Frame before you split.** Write who decides and what they decide, or for a purpose, what precision the answer needs. Write the undesired result if nothing changes, the desired result in end-product terms (a number or a recognisable end state), the constraints and the time frame. An unknown element gets a stated best assumption, never silence.

2. **Make it a choice.** List the real options: at least two, one of them carrying on as now, and a narrower variant where one exists. For each option write what would have to be true for it to be the right choice. Those conditions, not topics, are the tree.

3. **Hold a working answer and a rival.** State the working answer so that evidence could prove it wrong, and the strongest rival answer or explanation. Name the evidence that would separate them before planning any deep dive; a dive that cannot tell them apart runs last.

4. **Choose the top cut deliberately.** Try two or three ways to split the conditions: by the equation that produces the result, by the stages of a process or funnel, by options against common criteria, by competing causes, or by independent lines of evidence. For each, say what it reveals and what it hides. Pick the cut that would make a wrong answer easiest to disconfirm, and say why. Some fits recur: a "why is this falling short" question separates the normal level from the causes of shortfall before any remedy; a proposed change is tested on the conditions that must hold for it to work; a profit or cost question puts its controlling equation at the root; a sizing question starts from the population or customer funnel and converts to value afterwards; a choice among known options compares them on common criteria, never mixing options and criteria as siblings.

5. **One division principle per sibling group, at every depth.** State it in one line where the group appears. Siblings do not overlap and together cover what the decision needs; say what the group leaves out. When siblings can interact, state the rule that assigns each effect to exactly one of them. Deep levels are where overlap creeps in, so the deeper the group, the more explicit its principle.

6. **Write each node as a test.** A condition node carries its statement, how doubtful it is, what would settle it (the measurable variable or observable stand-in, and where it would come from) and its flip point: the value or finding at which the decision changes. A rival node carries the evidence that would make the rival win.

7. **Go deep where the answer lives.** Depth is uneven on purpose. A parent states what its children together imply, not a category label.

8. **Rank, then mark the crux.** Rank nodes by how much each could change the decision and how doubtful it is, and mark the one to three the decision turns on. Lower-ranked nodes are deferred, never dropped: for each, state the risk if the ranking is wrong and what would reopen it. Keep an open lane for primary-source reading nobody planned.

9. **Group leaves into lines of evidence.** Leaves that rest on the same sources become one line of work; the line, not the leaf, is what one worker takes.

10. **Check, and revise once if a check fails.** One level below the top: is each division principle stated, and is the separation real? Would settling the crux settle the decision? What finding would force a re-cut rather than a re-weighting? Write that last answer into frame.md.

Use no stock framework (SWOT, five forces, the four Ps) unless it fits this gap, and then say why. Give no branch equal depth for symmetry. The tree states what must be true; the orders say who looks where, so keep the two separate and let leaves change without re-approving the top.
