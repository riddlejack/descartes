# Framing and decomposing the question

Think the frame and tree through in prose in frame.md first; enter the tree with tree_upsert only once the prose holds together.

1. **Decompose the question asked.** The tree breaks the question, in the user's words and read narrowly unless the scope says otherwise, into the sub-questions that together answer it. The purpose says what form and precision the answer needs, never what else to research. A sub-question that matters only to a broader decision stays out of the tree and goes in the closing note on what was not researched.

2. **Let the question's type pick the frame.** A fact or forecast question gets an answer with its confidence. A "why" question gets a cause, separating the normal level from the causes of shortfall. A decision question gets options: fixed now when the user named them, with the tree's top parts as the shared criteria, each asked of every option, so that no part is one option's own terms; otherwise worked out after the research, from the findings. Recommend nothing the user did not ask for. A question that asks for a list or a ranking ("which are the most attractive") makes finding the candidates its first part, then how each is judged.

3. **Hold a working answer and its rival in frame.md, never in the tree.** State the working answer so that evidence could prove it wrong, and the strongest rival answer. While you build the tree, ask whether a sub-question exists whose answer would separate the two, and add it if it is missing. After the research, the rival is the adversary the draft is tested against.

4. **Settle the terms.** Write what each key term of the question means and, in one clause, why the definition matters. When a term has two reasonable readings and both are cheap to measure, research both and say which one the stated target or its source uses. Ask the user only when the readings would change the whole research; otherwise pick one and say so.

5. **Choose the top cut deliberately.** Try two or three ways to split the question: by the equation that produces the result, by the stages of a process or funnel, by competing causes, or by independent lines of evidence. For each, say what it reveals and what it hides. Pick the cut that would make a wrong answer easiest to disconfirm, and say why. Some fits recur: a profit or cost question puts its controlling equation at the root; a sizing question starts from the population or customer funnel and converts to value afterwards; a question whether a named change would work is tested on what must hold for it to.

6. **One division principle per sibling group, at every depth.** State it in one line where the group appears. Siblings do not overlap and together cover their parent question; say what the group leaves out. When siblings can interact, state the rule that assigns each effect to exactly one of them. Deep levels are where overlap creeps in, so the deeper the group, the more explicit its principle.

7. **Draw depth as sub-questions.** Each node is a question with how doubtful its answer is, what would settle it (the measurable variable or observable stand-in, and where it would come from) and the answer at which the headline would change. How a node will be answered becomes its child questions, such as which official series and on what basis, or what counts and what does not, never notes inside the node. A leaf asks for one measure; a leaf naming several measures that are answered separately becomes one leaf per measure. No option, rival, gate, independent check or work plan is a node; keep one open lane for primary-source reading nobody planned that bears on the question. A parent states what its children together imply, not a category label.

8. **Rank, set aside, then order.** Rank nodes by how much each could change the headline and how doubtful it is, and mark the one to three the answer turns on. Set a node aside only when neither of its possible answers could change the headline, and record why on the node; a node that is merely hard to answer is never set aside. Branches run in parallel unless one's value depends on another's answer; then hold it, and say so in its order.

9. **Group leaves into lines of evidence.** Leaves that rest on the same sources become one line of work; the line, not the leaf, is what one worker takes.

10. **Check, and revise once if a check fails.** Is every node part of the question asked, and is any part of it missing? Is each division principle stated, and is the separation real? Would settling the crux settle the answer? What finding would force a re-cut rather than a re-weighting? Write that last answer into frame.md.

Use no stock framework (SWOT, five forces, the four Ps) unless it fits this question, and then say why. Give no branch equal depth for symmetry. The tree states what must be answered; the orders say who looks where, so keep the two separate and let leaves change without re-approving the top.
